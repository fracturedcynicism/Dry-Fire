Part One: Six Chambers
---
CHAPTER 1. The Trunk Under the Rafters
The rain had been promising itself for three days. It sat over the laterite fields outside Shyamsundar the way a held breath sits in a throat, and the heat inside the house thickened to match it. Anirban Roy Chowdhury climbed into the attic a little after four in the afternoon with a torch between his teeth and a plastic bucket hooked over one wrist. He did not look at the trunk until he had counted the rafters.
Twenty-six. Then the gap where an old beam had split and been replaced with a sal log darker than the rest. Then the rest.
He had come to find the dalil. The lawyer in Bolpur needed the original deed for the sale of the eastern orchard, and the lawyer had said it in the voice people used once they had already decided what kind of man you were. The original, Mr Roy Chowdhury. The registrar does not accept photocopies. Surely you understand. He had said that he understood. He had understood a great many things in the last eleven months, most of them too late to be useful.
The trunk was teak, banded in brass gone green at the corners. The lid had warped so that it would not close even empty. Someone had painted R.R.C. on the front in white, and someone else had painted over the initials in black, and the black had cracked into the grain like the skin on an old hand. He knelt. Dust rose in a slow brown cloud, caught the torchlight, and hung there turning, as if it had nowhere else to go.
Inside were ledgers. Bundles of letters tied with cotton tape. A ceremonial sword in a wooden sheath, the lacquer lifting off in curls. A child's rattle made from a tin of Horlicks.
At the bottom, wrapped in four pages of a Bengali daily dated the ninth of March 1974, was something heavy enough to shift the whole trunk when he lifted it out.
He unwrapped it on the boards. The newsprint cracked. A headline about the price of kerosene. A photograph of a minister with his hands folded at his chest, like a man at prayer. Then the gun, lying on its side in the torchlight, its grip the colour of strong tea and its barrel a dull grey-blue where the bluing had worn thin at the muzzle.
A Webley. He knew that much because his father had kept a catalogue of British service arms on the shelf in Kolkata and had once shown him the page. Mark VI. .455. His father had said the numbers the way other men said cricket scores. Anir had been nine.
He picked it up. It was lighter than he had expected. The weight settled into the frame and the wrist accepted it without complaint, the way a hand accepts the hand of someone it has known a long time.
He should put it down. He knew this with the part of him that still wore a lanyard, that still signed off model validation reports with one line for Reviewed by and another for Approved by, that still believed in documentation. The other part, the part that had been waking at twenty to four every morning since March with a heartbeat against the inside of its ribs like something trying to get out of a locked room, did not believe in anything. It only wanted the weight to stay where it was.
He opened the breech. The cylinder swung out on its hinge with a dry click. Six chambers. Five rims had gone green with age. The sixth was clean and empty.
He closed it. In the attic the click was very loud.
Below him a voice called up through the floorboards. "Anir-babu. Khabar hoyeche."
Food is ready.
He folded the newspaper back around the gun, slowly, the corners tucked the way you fold a shirt you do not intend to wear. He put the bundle in the bucket beneath a towel and a stack of his father's pharmacy receipts, and he climbed down with the deed still missing and his hands not quite steady.

CHAPTER 2. Put It Back Where It Was
Sabitri had made dal with panch-phoron and a potato fried until it had gone soft and sweet at the edges. She set the thali on the cane table on the veranda and stood with her hands folded into the end of her sari while he sat.
"Bhola has gone to Bolpur for the bus," she said. "He'll be late."
"It's fine. I can eat alone."
"You don't eat alone. You eat sitting up, looking at the wall. I can see you from the kitchen." She said it without smiling and without unkindness, the way she might have remarked on the rain. She was past sixty and had cooked in this house for thirty-five years. She had outlived two husbands' worth of arguments about its roof. She had known Anir when he was a boy who ate raw mangoes with salt in the back courtyard and was caught every time by the sourness on his fingers.
He ate. The dal was thin at first and then, at the bottom of the bowl, rich, as if something had been added at the last minute. He chewed slowly and counted the chewing. Eleven. Twelve.
"Sabitri-di. Did my father keep papers up in the attic? The deed for the orchard?"
"Your father kept everything in the attic. Your grandfather kept everything in the cellar, but the cellar flooded in '98 and now it's bats." She tilted her head. "You found something."
"A gun."
She did not move. The bulb above the veranda was yellow and ringed with moths. The moths went on throwing themselves at it with the unhurried persistence of things that did not understand what a bulb was.
"Put it back," she said.
"It's old. It's a Webley. It isn't even—"
"Put it back where it was. Under the trunk."
"Why?"
She picked up his plate. Her bangles knocked against the steel. "Your grand-uncle did not die in the bamboo grove, whatever the register says. You don't need to know more than that tonight."
He opened his mouth. She shook her head once and went inside.
He sat with his hands on his knees. From the kitchen came the sound of water being poured over the sink, and then the scrape of a pan, and then nothing. A gecko on the wall above the door clicked twice and stopped. He waited for the second click to answer the first, the way it always did, and it didn't.
He went to the study early, before the light was gone from the courtyard. The room smelled of camphor and paper and something older underneath, a sweetness like fruit left too long in a bowl. He lay on the wooden cot with the ceiling fan turning above him at a speed that could not decide whether to be a fan or a clock. Its blades threw a shadow over the bookshelves, then off them, then back.
The phone on his chest lit up.
Lawyer's office, 9:30 am Monday. Bring original dalil. — M.
She had not written his name. She had written her initial, as though the message had been signed by a stranger she had once been introduced to at a wedding. He read it twice. He typed Can't find it yet. Ishu okay? and then deleted the second half of the sentence, and then deleted the first half, and then typed Yes and sent it, and watched the word sit there alone above the grey bubble of her reply, which came thirty seconds later.
Exam tomorrow. Don't call before 10.
He put the phone face-down on the sheet.
The layoff had come on a Wednesday, which he remembered only because the building's canteen served fish on Wednesdays and he had been thinking about the fish when the calendar invite arrived. Brief sync re: Lumen transition. 3 pm, Conf Room B. He had gone in with his laptop and a notebook and a cup of canteen tea going cold in his hand, and there had been three people in the room, and one of them was from HR and one of them was from the vendor, a young man in a blue shirt who kept touching the bridge of his glasses. The third was Sanjay Bhattacharya, who had hired him, who had once driven him home through a monsoon night because Anir's scooter had died in a puddle at Gariahat.
"You've been very thorough," Sanjay had said. He had said it kindly. That was what Anir kept returning to. Not the cruelty of it, which had not existed, but the kindness, which had been real.
"The Durgapur flags," he had said in June, standing at the window of Conference Room B with his laptop open on the sill. "Four hundred and twelve loans. Look at the amber. You can see the first slip in the amber, before it turns red."
Sanjay had not looked at the screen. "Lumen's already overridden the hold, Anir."
"On what basis?"
"Relationship coverage. Tier A."
"Nobody's Tier A is four hundred accounts in one mill cluster."
Sanjay had rubbed his eyes the way he did when a meeting ran late. "Write it up. Put it in the log. Let's see what the log says."
He had written it up. He had put the flags in red and the warning in amber, and he had sent the memo at 6:12 on a Thursday evening, and nobody had replied. In August, eleven of the four hundred and twelve had defaulted. The newspaper had called it a cascade. The bank had called it an unprecedented market event. Nobody asked for the log.
He had spent the next six weeks trying to find a sentence that began with I told you and did not sound like a man who had been right and wanted credit for it. He had not found one. He had found, instead, a set of numbers on his own desktop, timestamped, that proved he had been right, and a second set, also timestamped, that proved nobody had looked.
After that the house had come, and the lawyers, and the blue pen. Mitali had brought her own pen to the lawyer's office, a blue Pilot with the cap chewed at the top. She had signed the papers in one long stroke and then capped the pen and put it back in her bag and looked, for the first time in eleven years, directly at him. There had been nothing in her face that he could name. Not hatred. Not even sorrow. Only an acknowledgment, the kind a person gives to a stranger on a train whose station they have also missed.
He closed his eyes. The fan went around. Somewhere in the house a door opened and closed with the soft sigh of wood that had been swollen by a thousand monsoons.
He slept, and woke at 3:40 with his heart going, and lay still until he could count to fifty without losing the number.

CHAPTER 3. Four Steps Above a Roof That Isn't There
In the morning Bhola's bus had not come, and Sabitri had gone to her sister's in the next village, so the house was his. He walked through it the way a man walks a property he is about to sell, trying to see it through the eyes of a buyer and failing.
The main hall ran the length of the east side, its floor of Italian tiles gone the colour of wet sand. A ceiling fan hung from a rod that someone had painted silver in the seventies. Along the north wall, in a row of carved teak frames, the family looked down at him: his great-grandfather in a dhoti with a gold watch-chain across his belly; his grandmother with a tilt to her head that suggested she had been asked to smile and had declined; his grandfather in a three-piece suit that had been tailored for a man who did not sweat. The grandfather's portrait hung at the centre. The glass was cracked diagonally from one corner to the other, and the crack ran through the grandfather's left eye and out across his cheek.
Beside the hall was the thakur ghar, the family shrine, a room no bigger than a bathroom with a brass lamp on a stand and a marble idol of Ganesh with its trunk worn smooth by generations of thumbs. The air inside was cooler. He stood in the doorway and did not step in. Someone had left a wilted marigold on the threshold and it had gone the brown of old paper.
The study was at the back. The grain store was beyond it, then the kitchen, then the stairs to the attic, then the courtyard and the eastern wing, which was closed off by a wooden door with a brass padlock the size of a fist. The padlock had no dust on it. Someone had oiled it recently.
He stood in front of that door for a long time.
Somewhere above him, in a part of the roof that did not exist, something moved across the boards. Four steps, unhurried. A pause. Four steps back.
He looked up. The ceiling was white and undamaged. There was nothing above the east wing but sky and the ruined stone of an older building, the one his grandfather had walled over and never finished. He knew this. He had measured it himself as a boy, pacing it out with a rope, and had lain awake that night because the house had seemed suddenly larger than the land it stood on.
The steps came again. Four, pause, four.
He went back to the study. He shut the door. He sat on the cot and put his hands flat on his thighs and breathed in for four, held for four, out for four, and when his pulse had come down to a rate he could count without flinching, he stood, and went to the almirah, and took out the newspaper bundle from its hiding place under the stack of ledgers.
He had been dry-firing the gun for three nights.
It had started by accident. He had found a manual in the bookshelf, a faded Indian Army handbook for the Webley with the cover half-torn, and had read it in bed by torchlight, and somewhere in the paragraph on trigger control he had found himself picking up the empty revolver and pulling the trigger. The hammer fell. The click was small and clean and utterly final, and something in his chest had loosened, the way a fist loosens when it is finally allowed to open.
He did it now. He stood at the study window with the blind half up and the courtyard light coming in grey and thin. He opened the breech. He checked the chambers, all six, the way the manual said: empty, empty, empty, empty, empty, empty. He closed the breech. He lifted the gun, both hands, the front sight on the frame of the window where the mosquito net was torn. He began to breathe.
In for four.
He pulled the trigger.
The hammer fell. Click.
Hold for four.
He drew the trigger back again, slowly, through the long heavy resistance that the manual called stacking, and felt the sear catch and release, and the hammer fell.
Click.
Out for four.
From the corridor outside the study, from somewhere past the grain store, a second click answered. Small. Metallic. Exactly the same.
He did not move. He did not breathe. The gun stayed up, the front sight steady on the torn net. He counted in his head. One, two, three, four. Then he lowered the gun.
The corridor was empty. The light was there. A ring of dust moved in the slant of it. He went to the door and opened it and looked out at the long dim hall with its portraits and its cool tiles and nothing else.
"Bhola?" he called.
No answer. He stood there another minute, then pulled the door shut and went back to the cot and sat down heavily, and noticed that his left hand was holding the gun by the barrel, the way you hold a thing you no longer have a use for.

CHAPTER 4. The Red Book
The ledger had been under the almirah, flat against the back panel where the wood had split. He found it the next afternoon while looking for the old cash box, which had been used to pay the village labourers and was, he had been told, the only item in the house with a working lock.
It was the household account book, the bahi-khata, bound in red cloth, the spine stitched in a thick black thread that had gone soft and brown. Each page was ruled into columns in faded blue ink: Date. Particulars. Jama. Kharcha. Balance. The entries were in Bengali and in an English that had been learned from a Presbyterian school in Burdwan and never fully forgiven. Rice, forty-two seers, four rupees eight annas. Oil, lamp, two bottles. Repair, eastern gate, mason's fee. Paid to Kalitola, the ferryman, in full.
He turned the pages slowly, the way he had once turned through a loan book, scanning for the place where a pattern breaks. The entries were regular for decades. Then, in the spring of 1952, the handwriting changed. It became smaller, more careful, and the columns grew longer, with more words and fewer numbers.
14 Aug 1952. Cr. 455. Shyamal. Kalitola. Settled.
He read the line four times. Then he turned the page, and the next entry was in a different ink, blacker and newer, though it was not new at all.
Balance: nil.
Folded into the back cover was a photograph. Three brothers, stiff in white dhotis and buttoned shirts, posed in front of a painted backdrop of a garden that did not exist. The eldest had his hand on the shoulder of the youngest. The middle one stood a little apart, half a step back, his face turned slightly toward the camera as if he had heard someone call his name and was about to answer. Someone had taken a steel nib to the middle face. The lines had gone deep into the paper, dozens of short fierce strokes, until nothing of the eyes or the mouth or the brow remained except a grey scar, the shape of a man with no face.
Anir held it to the window. The light came through the scratches like light through a sieve.
On the back someone had written, in the same careful hand as the 1952 entries, a single word, and then a number.
Shyamal. 455.
He put the photograph back in the ledger, and the ledger in the almirah, and went to find Bhola, who had come back in the night and was sitting on the back step with his feet in a bucket of water, smoking a bidi and looking at the pukur as though it owed him money.
"The register," Anir said. "The one for the family. Where is it kept?"
Bhola took a long time to answer. He shifted his feet in the bucket. Then he shook his head slowly. "Your father burned it."
"He didn't."
"He did. In the year he sold the cattle shed. I was there. He put it in the stove and sat next to it until it was all black." Bhola drew on the bidi. "He was crying. Not much. The way old men do."
"What did it say?"
"I didn't read it, Anir-babu. Why would I read it?" He looked up at Anir with eyes gone yellow at the rims. "I know what happened to Shyamal Roy Chowdhury because everyone in Kalitola knows. The ferryman's son still won't touch that water. They say a boy cannot swim across the pukur without the hand of a dead man pulling his ankle. That's what they say." He shrugged. "I don't believe it. I believe in the bank of the pukur, where the mud is soft."
Anir sat on the step beside him. The water in the bucket was cool and the bidi smoke went straight up into the still air.
"Do you ever hear things in the east wing?" he asked.
Bhola was quiet. Then he said, "Every night, Anir-babu. But you should not sleep in the study. Sleep in the front room, by the portrait. Ghosts don't like to be too close to the glass."
He laughed, a short ugly sound, and Bhola did not laugh with him.
CHAPTER 5. Oil, Screws, and a Daughter on the Line
That evening he cleaned the gun.
He did it on the study floor on a bedsheet, with the veranda bulb's light falling through the open door and the gecko watching from the wall. He had found a tin of gun oil in the bucket with the newspaper, and a brass bore brush, and a screwdriver in his father's shaving kit with a handle wrapped in black tape. He worked by the manual, which was open beside him, its pages gone soft and translucent at the folds.
The oil smelled of nothing he could name except time. He worked it into the frame, the hinge, the edge of the cylinder where it met the frame, and the metal began to shine under the bulb: a dark blue that became, under the rag, almost black. The grip panels were cracked along one edge. He ran his thumb over the cracks and felt them catch on his skin.
Halfway through, the phone rang.
Ishu. He looked at the screen, at the small picture of his daughter at eight, squinting into a monsoon sun, and his thumb hovered over the green circle for a second longer than it should have.
"Baba?" Her voice was thick and far away, as if she were calling from the bottom of a well. "Are you okay? Ma said you were in the village."
"I'm in the village."
"You sound like you're underwater."
"The line's bad. Are you studying?"
"I'm not studying. I'm eating kulfi on the balcony and Ma doesn't know." A pause. He could hear traffic, a horn, a dog. Then, quieter: "Did you find the thing? The paper?"
"Not yet."
"Okay." Another pause, longer. "Baba, do you still do the thing? The counting?"
He looked at the gun on the sheet, its parts laid out in a neat line like the items of a surgeon's tray. Frame. Cylinder. Hammer. Trigger. Side plate. Grips. The screws in a small paper twist.
"Sometimes," he said.
"Okay," she said again, and he could hear that she was trying to keep the word light, and that she was failing, and that she had been failing for a long time without his noticing. "Call me tomorrow, then. After ten. Ma says you can call after ten."
"After ten."
"Love you, Baba."
She hung up before he could answer. He held the phone against his cheek until the screen went dark.
He did not go back to the gun for an hour. He went out to the pukur instead, down the broken steps in the failing light, past the dead jackfruit tree and the shrine of a snake-goddess with her paint worn to pale pink. The water was still. A kingfisher sat on a rope of coir tied to a stake and did not move when he passed. The mud at the edge was soft, as Bhola had said, and it took the imprint of his shoes and held it.
He stood on the lowest step and looked into the water.
His reflection looked back up at him, dim and rippled and mostly sky. Behind it, the edge of the bank, the green slope of the field, the orange smear of the sunset. Above all of it, the house, with its rectangle of yellow light in the study window and its dark eastern wing.
He saw himself lift a hand.
The reflection did not lift a hand.
For a second, a long one, he simply stood there with his arm in the air and waited for it to catch up. It did not. The water did not move. The kingfisher did not move. Then his reflection lifted its hand, slowly, a fraction late, the way a bad film is out of sync with its soundtrack, and he let his arm fall and stepped back so hard that the soft mud gave under his heel and he sat down on the steps with his heart slamming in his ears.
Far off, beyond the paddy and the mango groves, the meter-gauge train from Bolpur blew its horn at the level crossing. It was a thin, uncertain sound, like something bleating in a dream. He counted the seconds until it stopped.
Eleven.
When he looked back at the water, his reflection was gone, and there was only the house.

CHAPTER 6. Six Things That Can Be Lost
The power went at nine. It did not flicker first. It simply stopped, the fan slowing in its long shudder to nothing, the bulb on the veranda dying with a small orange sigh. The gecko's clicking stopped. In the silence he could hear the blood in his own ears, and under that, very faint, the soft drip of the kitchen tap.
He found a hurricane lamp in the kitchen, its glass blackened at one side, and a box of matches on the shelf above the stove that had been damp and then dried and crackled when struck. He lit the wick and turned it down low and carried it back to the study.
The gun was waiting on the sheet where he had left it. Its parts were gleaming faintly in the lamplight.
He sat cross-legged in front of them. The manual was open at the diagram of the Webley's internal mechanism, a line drawing with dotted arrows and small numbered callouts, and he read the callouts over and over without taking any of them in, the way he used to read a validation report the night before a review, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something acceptable.
Remove side plate. Remove hammer and mainspring. Remove trigger and sear. Remove cylinder. Remove frame from grip.
He did it in that order. The side plate came away on the third screw, with a little tick, like a tooth slipping out of its socket. Behind it the mechanism was revealed: a tangle of steel, the hammer cocked back, the mainspring coiled tight as a held breath. He eased the hammer forward with his thumb and the spring unwound and he caught it in his palm before it could fly into the dark. He set the hammer down. He set the mainspring beside it. The trigger came out with a single small pull. The sear, a tiny angled piece of steel no longer than his smallest fingernail, sat in his palm like a grain of rice.
The cylinder came off the crane with a push of the thumb. He held it up to the lamp and turned it slowly. The five green-rimmed chambers. The sixth, clean and dark, a black hole with nothing in it.
He held the empty chamber to his eye and looked through it at the flame.
Then he put the cylinder down and took off the grips, which came away in two cracked halves, and last of all he took the frame, the long heavy spine of the thing, and set it on the bedsheet. It looked, now, like something that had been killed and laid out on a table to be examined. A part without a whole. A body without a voice.
He sat back on his heels and let the lamp hiss.
A part of him, a very cold part that had always been good at its job, began to think about where to put things.
He had worked his whole adult life inside systems where nothing was ever lost; it was only misfiled. Every loan had an identifier. Every default had a date. The trick was not to hide information but to make it so ordinary that no one would look twice. A column of numbers in an accounts ledger could carry any message at all, if you knew how to read the margin.
He opened the bahi-khata on the floor beside the lamp. The red cloth was warm. He turned past his grandfather's neat accounts, past the rice and the oil and the repairs, to the first clean page after the entries stopped, and he took the fountain pen from the drawer of the almirah and tested it on the back of his hand. It wrote.
He began with the lamp.
The thakur ghar was the first place his mind went, and he let it go there. He wrapped the side plate and its four screws in a square of the newspaper with the minister's praying hands, and he carried the bundle into the little shrine with the lamp held high in his other hand. The idol of Ganesh watched him with its worn smooth trunk and its stained eyes. The brass oil lamp stood on its stand beneath the idol. He lifted the lamp's cup, which came off easily, and laid the bundle in the hollow of the stand, then set the cup back on top so that nothing was out of place, and he thought, as he did it, of how little anyone looked at a shrine they did not already believe in.
He returned to the study and wrote in the book.
Cr. 1. 1 Sept. Rice, nil.
He stopped, pen hovering. Then he went on writing, the way he would have written a line in a forecast.
Cr. 3. Brass. Silent.
The cylinder he wrapped in an old page of the ledger itself, a page of a year he could not read because the ink had run in the damp, and he carried it to the almirah. The almirah was tall and dark and had a mirror set into its back panel, a sheet of cloudy glass that gave back the room as if from underwater. He slid the cylinder behind the mirror's frame, into the gap between the glass and the wood, and felt it settle with a faint knock. The sound was very small. Then nothing.
Cr. 455.
He looked at that line for a long time.
The hammer and the mainspring went into the grain store, where the rice husks had been stacked in a dented tin bin that smelled of dust and rot and mouse. He buried the wrapped steel in the husks at the bottom, then pressed the husks down with his palm until the surface looked as undisturbed as the rest. He wrote that down. It was a simple entry. He did not add any words to it.
The trigger and the sear he took into the hall. The grandfather's portrait hung above the sideboard, its cracked glass catching the lamp in a thin white line. He stood in front of it and looked at the stern face, the cracked eye, the gold watch-chain. Then he lifted the frame from its nail, very carefully, and tilted it, and slid the two tiny pieces of steel behind the backing board where the cardboard had come away from the wood. When he hung the portrait back on its nail it swung a little, and then it was still, and the grandfather looked down at him through the crack as though he had known all along where the pieces were going.
The grips were harder. They were too big for any of the spaces he had chosen and too cracked to be trusted with anything wet. He carried them to the old clock in the hall, a tall mahogany case with a dial that had not told the right time since the Emergency. The brass bell inside the case was hollow and the bronze hammer that struck it had long since rusted into place. He opened the clock's door, reached in, and felt the cold metal of the bell, and then he slid the cracked grips in behind it, one and then the other, and closed the door, and the clock did not tick.
He did not write the grips down yet. He would come back to them.
The frame was last, and the frame was the one that frightened him.
It was too heavy for a hiding place that could be opened by a child. It was too long for anything but the deepest space in the house. He thought about the pukur and rejected it at once: water would ruin the springs, and the springs were what made the parts into a gun. He thought about the courtyard and rejected that too, because the courtyard was open to the sky and the sky could see.
Then he remembered Bhola's words, the cellar flooded in '98 and now it's bats, and he lifted the lamp and went down the narrow stone stairs at the back of the kitchen.
The air changed as he descended. It went from dry to damp to something heavier, close and sweet with the smell of guano and decaying wood. The stairs ended in a low vaulted chamber, its floor covered in a few inches of grey silt that had dried into cracked plates. The walls were streaked with white mineral deposits. In the far corner was a heap of broken bottles, a rusted iron bed frame, a wooden chair with three legs. Bats hung from the vault in a dense black cluster that shifted and whispered as the lamp came near.
He set the lamp on the bottom step and carried the frame across the silt, placing each foot carefully on the cracked plates. He laid the frame in the gap behind the iron bed frame, where the wall curved in and the rubble had piled up in a sheltered niche, and pushed a broken board over it. The board was rotten and light. It would hold for a while. It would not hold for ever.
He stood in the cellar with the bats whispering above him and the lamp's light trembling on the walls, and he waited for his heartbeat to slow, and it did not.
Cr. 455 (frame). Cellar. Dry.
He wrote it on the stairs, leaning against the wall with the ledger open on his knee. His hand was shaking, so the figures came out crooked, the four lifting into the five like a man losing his footing on a step. He looked at the crooked figures and then, slowly, wrote the amount again beneath them, correctly.
CHAPTER 7. The Sum Closes
Then he sat on the stone step and did the sums.
It was not difficult. The lines were all there. Six entries, six places. Each place a number, each number a little deviation from the last. He added them in his head, the way he had added a thousand columns of loan balances over the years, and he checked the total, and checked it again, and got a figure that did not close to anything. He tried the same numbers in the other order, the way a man tries a key in a lock that he knows is wrong, and got the same figure. He tried the cylinder's number subtracted from the total, and then divided by the number of chambers, and got a clean remainder of zero.
He sat very still.
Balance: nil.
He had not planned that. He realised it only when he saw the words, in the faded blue ink of his own hand, at the bottom of the page. He had written it automatically, as he had written it in every ledger he had ever closed.
Above him, in the kitchen, something fell with a soft wooden clatter. A pot lid, perhaps. Or a board.
He got up. He took the lamp. He climbed the stairs slowly, pausing at each step, listening. The kitchen was dark. The lid of the rice pot lay on the floor by the sink, its brass handle catching the lamp. Nothing else had moved.
He went to the front of the house. Through the jalousie of the front room he saw the road, a pale strip under the moon, and a line of trees beyond it, and beyond them the faint red wink of the signal tower at the Bolpur junction. Nothing was on the road.
Then, far off, a vehicle's headlights swept across the top of the trees, slowly, the way headlights move when the car is looking for something. The light came closer. It fell across the wall of the house, through the jalousie, and laid a long white bar of brightness over the cracked portrait of the grandfather, over the brass lamp in the shrine across the hall, over the lamp in his own hand.
He did not breathe.
The light lingered. Then it moved on, slowly, down the road toward the crossing, and the engine's sound faded into the night until it was gone.
He stood there with the lamp in his hand until he could count to a hundred. Then he went back into the study and sat down on the cot and put his face in his hands and said his daughter's name out loud, once, to hear how it sounded in the room.

CHAPTER 8. Before the Light
He left before the light came up.
He packed one bag. A shirt, two pairs of trousers, the phone charger, the torch. He did not take the manual. He did not take the ledger either, though he stood by the almirah for a long time with his hand on its red cloth cover before he closed the door and turned the old key and put the key into the pocket of his jeans, and then took the key out again and put it on the study table, under the lamp's dead glass, where anyone who opened the room would see it.
On the first page of the ledger, below the printed heading and the faded columns, he wrote a single line in English, in the same careful hand as the 1952 entries. He wrote it so that it could be read by someone who had never been in this room.
Read the margins. The sum closes.
He did not sign it.
Outside, the courtyard was grey. Mist lay on the pukur in a thin white sheet, and the dead jackfruit tree was a black scratch against the sky. The kingfisher was gone. The snake-goddess in her shrine had her hands raised, palms outward, in the posture of a woman telling a crowd to be still.
He walked around the house once, the long way, past the cattle shed and the grain store with its rotten door and the eastern wing with its oiled brass padlock. The padlock was cold under his fingers. He did not try it. He knew it would not give. He pressed his palm flat against the wood of the door for a moment, the way a man might rest his hand on the flank of a horse he is about to leave, and then he took it away.
Above the eastern wing, the roof stayed quiet. No steps. No pause.
He did not know whether that was better or worse.
At the gate he stopped, because he had heard something behind him in the dew. He turned.
The veranda was empty. The cane table, the cane chairs, the thali still stacked on the step with its ring of dried dal. The bulb above the door was off. Its glass caught the first grey light and gave it back dull and milky, like a single eye.
Behind the glass of the bulb, very faint, something moved.
He stood with the bag over his shoulder and watched the empty veranda until the grey light grew strong enough to show that the room was empty and only the bulb was there.
Then he turned and walked out onto the dirt road and did not look back.
The road ran straight east for two kilometres toward the railway line. The laterite was red-brown and crumbling at the edges, and the fields on either side were flooded in patches where the rain had come early in the north. He walked through the mist with his shoes filling with grey water and the bag bumping against his hip. A cart passed with a bullock, the driver asleep on the front board, his head nodding in time with the animal's slow tread. A woman at a well looked up at him and then away. A boy with a stick drove three goats in front of him along the verge, and the goats stopped and looked at Anirban Roy Chowdhury with their yellow indifferent eyes, and then went on.
He came to the railway embankment just as the sun cleared the mango groves. The line ran north and south across the flat land, two thin steel rails laid on wooden sleepers, narrower than any train in Kolkata. Meter gauge, his father had called it once, on a holiday, pointing out the gap between the rails with the tip of his umbrella. Narrow enough to get you home. Not wide enough to get you anywhere else.
He stepped up onto the track bed and walked along the rails, placing one foot on each side of the steel, the way he had once walked along the same rails as a boy, daring himself to keep his balance for a hundred steps.
He counted. One. Two. Three.
The mist was lifting from the fields. A heron stood in a flooded paddy with its neck folded into its shoulders. Far ahead, where the line curved toward Bolpur, a light appeared on the rails, small and white and wavering, and the sound of an engine came through the cold air like something breathing under water.
He stopped walking and stood very still, his feet on either side of the steel, and listened to the train come.
The horn sounded, once, thin and uncertain. He did not move. He counted the seconds until the horn sounded again.
Eleven.
Then he stepped off the rail, onto the soft wet grass of the embankment, and let the train go past him in a roar of iron and steam and the faces of strangers turning, one by one, to look out at the man standing alone on the edge of the field, his bag at his feet, his hands loose at his sides, a little too still, like a man who has just pulled a trigger on an empty chamber and is waiting, with the whole of his body, for the sound.
