Part Two: Reassembly
---
CHAPTER 1. The Meter Gauge
The meter-gauge train from Howrah came into Shyamsundar eleven minutes late, which the guard said was punctual by the standards of the Bolpur line. Ishita Roy Chowdhury stood on the platform with a canvas duffel on one shoulder and a leather toolkit under her arm, and watched the carriages sigh to a stop, and waited for the doors to open on a village that had not, in eleven years, appeared on any map she had been shown.
Nobody got off with her. Two women with a basket of dried fish. A boy with a bicycle that had no seat. The guard lifted his flag and looked at her once, the way people looked at a girl who had come alone to a place where nothing was supposed to happen.
The platform was cracked concrete under a tin roof on iron posts, with a single bulb burning in a wire cage. A tea stall had its shutter half down. A dog slept in the shadow of a water tank. Beyond the tracks the land went flat and red-brown under the late sun, the laterite broken into plates, the paddy standing in shallow water that threw back the sky in grey strips.
She had expected the heat. She had not expected the silence after the train, so complete she could hear the bulb's electrical hum from twenty metres off.
Don't do the counting, her mother had said on the phone two nights earlier. Your father counted everything. Doors. Stairs. The seconds between lightning and thunder. People do it when they're frightened, Ishu. It doesn't help.
I'm not frightened.
You were always a bad liar. Don't start now with a house you've never seen.
At the ticket gate a man in a khaki shirt asked her name, wrote it in a register, and asked whether she was the daughter of Anirban Roy Chowdhury. She said yes. He wrote something after the name and did not look up.
A bullock cart waited outside, its driver asleep under a palm-leaf hat. When she woke him he studied her face for a long time before he said Roy Chowdhury Bari, not as a question. The cart creaked onto a road of crushed laterite toward a line of mango trees. Somewhere in the trees a koel started calling, the same two notes over and over, pitched like a question nobody intended to answer.
---
The house appeared at the end of the road the way old houses do in flat country, all at once: a long low mass of plaster and brick and tile that seemed to have risen out of the earth rather than been built on it. A veranda ran the length of the front. Cane chairs. A cane table. A bulb above the door, unlit, its glass grey with dust. The paint on the columns had flaked into continents, and the brick beneath was the colour of dried blood.
An old man sat on the top step with a towel over his shoulders. He stood when the cart stopped, one hand on the column, and came down one step at a time. When he reached the gravel his face crumpled and then re-formed into something close to a smile.
"Ishu-didi," Bhola said.
She had last seen him at ten. She had remembered him as tall. He was not tall now. He had shrunk into his clothes, and the left side of his face did not move the way the right did, and his left hand trembled when he took her duffel.
"Bhola-kaka. You're thinner."
"I'm old. Thinness is what's left." His eyes went to the road, the tree line, back to her, quick and careful, like a man checking for a bird he expected to swoop down. "The lawyer sent you."
"The registrar needs an identity check for the sale. I signed the form in Kolkata. He said someone from the family had to come and stand in the room."
"The lawyer is always vague. It's how he gets paid." Bhola turned toward the house. "Come in before the light goes. There are things you should see."
Inside, the hall was cool. The tiles had been sand-coloured once and had worn to a dull shine where feet had walked for a century. A fan hung motionless from the ceiling. Along the north wall the family looked down at her from carved teak frames: her great-grandfather with a gold watch-chain across his belly; her grandmother with her head tilted, as if someone had asked her to smile and she had declined politely; her grandfather in a three-piece suit, at the centre, under a crack that ran through the glass and across his left eye.
She had seen these faces in her father's album, the one she had found in the Kolkata flat after the papers were signed and the furniture divided.
She stopped in front of the grandfather. He did not look back. He looked past her shoulder toward the door.
"He used to stand there," Bhola said quietly. "Your father. Every morning when he came in from the pukur. He stood exactly there and looked up at the old man, like he was checking the time."
"Why?"
Bhola did not answer. He went on toward the study.
---
The study smelled of camphor and paper and, underneath, something sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in a bowl. A cot against one wall. A table with a hurricane lamp, its glass blackened on one side and clean on the other. Ledgers on the shelves, their spines faded to the colour of old bone.
On the table, under the lamp's dead glass, lay a brass key on a folded sheet of paper. The paper had been placed so precisely that the table's edge ran parallel to its fold, the way a man leaves an object he has measured.
"He left that," Bhola said. "Eleven years ago. He put it there and went out. I came back from the bus and the lamp was still warm."
She unfolded the paper. It was a sheet torn from the back of a ledger, ruled in faded blue, and her father had written a line on it in English, in the careful hand that had once signed her birthday cards.
Read the margins. The sum closes.
No signature. Below the line, in pencil, very small: a tick mark. Then another.
She felt the old tightening under her ribs, the thing her mother called Baba's disease, the thing that made him count and made her hate counting. She set the paper down face-up and put the key in her pocket.
"Who else has been in this room?"
"Nobody," said Bhola. "I dust it. I don't move the paper."
CHAPTER 2. Oil on the Hinge
She did not sleep much that first night. At two in the morning she walked across the courtyard with the key in her hand toward the eastern wing.
The door was shut and bolted with a brass padlock the size of a fist. The shackle shone. Pale dust lay on every surface of the courtyard stones and on the sill of the door, but the padlock's loop was bright and clean, the way metal looks when someone has worked oil into it not long ago.
She put the key in. It did not turn. She had known it would not. She stood with her forehead against the cool wood and waited, and nothing moved on the other side.
Then, from above the wing, from a roof that the sky seemed to hold up on its own: four steps. Unhurried. A pause. Four steps back.
She turned away and walked to the back door without looking up, and her shoes made soft sounds on the stones, and she did not count.
---
CHAPTER 3. Cr. 1, Rice, Nil
Morning came up grey and wet over the mango trees. Ishita had decided in the night what she would do, the way she decided on a failing lab rig: take it apart in order, document every piece, and do nothing she could not undo.
She began in the grain store.
The rice husks had settled into a dense brown bed at the bottom of a dented tin bin. The surface was flat and undisturbed, the surface of something somebody had pressed down with a palm and walked away from.
Cr. 1. Rice, nil.
She had read the line a dozen times by the lamp in the study. It had sat on the page like a stone in a river, the water going around it. She had made the mistake, at first, of reading the entries in order, down the page, Cr. 1, Cr. 3, Cr. 455, then a blank, then the frame. Reading from the bottom up had made it obvious.
The first line was not the first thing he had hidden. It was the first thing he had written. He had written the grain store before the shrine, because the grain store was the place he had been thinking about while he sat at the table, the place where husks were thick enough to hide anything. He had labelled it Rice, nil because the bin was empty of rice, and the nil was not a mistake. It was a joke he had been telling himself. She could see him doing it: a man who used to close every column with nil at three in the morning, grinning at his own private accounting.
She put her hand into the husks and dug.
Steel, cold and heavy, wrapped in newspaper gone soft with damp. She drew it out and the husks slid from its folds in a brown rain. She unwrapped it on the concrete under her torch.
A hammer, blued and heavy, its face worn bright where a thumb had rested. Beside it, coiled tight in an oily spiral, a mainspring as thick as her little finger.
She sat back on her heels. She had spent four years in the machine lab with her hands in gears and springs and the flat, exact language of fits and tolerances. A hammer stored energy and released it all at once. A spring supplied the energy. The whole assembly existed for one purpose: to fall at exactly the right moment. A hammer without its spring was dead weight. A spring without a hammer was wire waiting for a job.
She wrapped both in a clean cloth and put them in the leather toolkit beside her.
---
In the hall she stood under her grandfather's portrait with the toolkit at her feet and looked at the crack. It ran from the top corner of the frame, through the left eye, down across the cheek. Behind the glass the cardboard backing had come loose at one corner, the way cardboard does after decades of monsoon damp.
Left eye.
Her father's pencil note, in the ledger, under the 1952 entry. She had read it last night and not understood it. She understood it now.
She lifted the portrait from its nail, carefully, the way he must have lifted it. It weighed more than she expected. She turned it over on the tiles, worked her fingers under the loose corner of the cardboard, and the backing came away with a small sigh of old paper.
Pressed flat against the teak, wrapped in a square of faded cloth, was a thin dark shape.
She unwrapped it. The trigger and sear lay in her palm, small and cold and very exact. They weighed almost nothing.
Behind them, folded flat against the wood, was a second sheet of paper, yellowed at the edges, its ink gone brown. She did not unfold it in the hall. She put it inside her shirt and went to the study, and bolted the door, and sat on the cot with the portrait's back still open beside her, and read it under the lamp.
It was a counterfoil. A receipt stub, cut from a book of the kind the Kalitola ferry family had used for toll collections, with the perforated edge still ragged. The text was in Bengali, in a fine careful hand she did not recognise, dated the twentieth of July, 1952:
Received from the house of Roy Chowdhury, through the hand of R.R.C., five hundred rupees, in full settlement for services at Kalitola, for the land and ferry rights. Clear title to follow.
Below it: H. Mondal, signed in a blocky hand.
Below that, in a different ink, in a shakier hand, the grandfather's:
The shot was true. The old man will not speak again.
And at the bottom, very small, a second note in pencil. Her father's hand.
Mondal has the original. This is ours. Keep both.
She sat very still.
---
The screwdriver was in the almirah, in a drawer lined with old newspaper. Her father's shaving kit lay beside it, a battered tin with a dented lid and a brush gone bald. The screwdriver's handle was wrapped in black tape that had gone sticky with age. She put it in her pocket.
The cylinder was behind the mirror. She had worked it out from the first ledger line, and she had been afraid to touch it. She stood in front of the tall dark almirah for a long time with her hand on the carved handle before she opened the door. The mirror at the back gave her back her own face, pale and narrow, a sheet of cloudy glass with a face drowning under it.
Cr. 455.
She worked her fingers into the gap between the frame and the wood, and felt for the edge of the cylinder, and it came free with a small dry knock. Cold. Heavier than she expected.
She held it to the window. Five chambers with brass rims gone green. The sixth clean and dark and empty.
She closed her eyes and heard her father in this room saying five, five, five, the way he used to count mango trees on the road in summer, as if the number mattered more than the trees.
She put the cylinder in the toolkit.
---
The shrine was next. The room was cooler than the hall and smelled of old marigolds. The idol of Ganesh watched her with its trunk worn smooth by generations of thumbs. The brass oil lamp stood on its stand beneath it.
She lifted the lamp's cup, which came off like a lid, and under it the hollow of the stand held a square of newspaper. Inside the newspaper, the side plate and its four screws, wrapped in a twist of paper.
Cr. 3. Brass. Silent.
She took the screws out of the twist one by one and counted them, four, and felt the old reflex in her chest, and let it go. Counting was for people who were not doing anything.
CHAPTER 4. Four Seventeen
The grips were behind the brass bell inside the hall clock.
The mahogany case stood against the wall beside the sideboard, tall and dark, its dial stopped at seventeen minutes past four. She opened the glass door and reached in past the pendulum and found the bell, cold and heavy, and behind it, slid into the gap between the bell and the case, two cracked panels of dark wood worn smooth.
Pencil on the inside of the case, faint, almost lost in the grain: 4:17.
Her father had marked it. He had gone back to the clock after he wrote the ledger, to be certain of the place. It was the kind of certainty he had always wanted, the kind that made him sit up at night with a calculator.
She took the panels out and laid them in the toolkit with the rest.
---
The frame was the last, and she did not want to go down into the cellar.
The stone steps smelled of guano and wet earth. The vault was low, the silt on the floor dried into cracked grey plates. Bats hung in a dense black cluster and shifted with a faint whispering sound when the torch came near. She went across the silt with each foot placed carefully, the way one walks on ice, toward the corner where the old iron bed frame lay against the wall.
She saw the broken board at once. It had been pushed over a shallow niche in the stone, dry and light. Her father had chosen the spot because he knew nobody would look under a rotten board in a flooded cellar. She lifted it with one hand. It came away in crumbs.
The oilcloth was still there. She unwrapped it in the torchlight and the blued steel of the frame gleamed through a film of dust, the trigger guard intact, the hinge pin in place, the long spine of the thing cool as river stone.
Cr. 455 (frame). Cellar. Dry.
It was dry. He had been right about that. She could have wept at how right he had been.
She climbed the stairs with the frame under one arm and the toolkit on her shoulder, and when she reached the kitchen Bhola was sitting on the stool by the stove, looking at her as though she had climbed out of a grave.
"You found it," he said.
"You knew it was there."
"I knew where he put things. That's not the same." He reached for the teapot with his good hand. "Sit down, Ishu-didi. I'll tell you what I know. It isn't much."
---
He told her over tea, the kerosene stove ticking and the bananas on the shelf going yellow at the tips.
"Two weeks back," Bhola said, "a black car came up that road. Three men. One had a thin clerk with a file under his arm and a pen behind his ear." He set the cup down on the stove's iron rim. "They said Mondal Infra. They had papers with stamps on them. They asked about the orchard first. Then they asked about the gun, though not one of them said the word."
"What did you tell them?"
"That I'm a poor old servant." The corner of his mouth moved. "They believed it. It was the only true thing I said all afternoon."
"And the clerk?"
"The clerk said the thing from the attic," Bhola said. "He said the family had taken something from Kalitola a long time ago, and it should come home. He said the house would be left alone if it came home. He said the bank would not look too closely at the old title if the old thing was gone."
"The bank."
"That's what he said. They are building a road. The road goes through Kalitola. The bank is paying for the road, and the bank has a man who checks every document before the money moves." Bhola drank. "I don't understand banks. Your father did."
Ishita thought of the blue ink on a ledger, of a column with a reason code beside it that nobody had questioned. She said nothing.
"And the receipt?" she asked. "The Mondals have the original."
"The Mondals have always had the original. It's their insurance. Seventy years, they keep it in their own house, and every time somebody in Kalitola talks too loud, they make sure the Roy Chowdhury family hears the name Haren Mondal." Bhola looked at the stove. "Nobody knew the old man kept the other half. That's the thing. A man who pays for a killing keeps the stub so the one who did it can't take the other half to the police and say you were paid. Your grandfather knew that. He kept a copy of the payment in his own portrait, where no one would look. He was a careful man, too."
"Careful."
"Careful men are the ones who keep everything." Bhola set down his cup. "Your father was careful. He wrote everything down. I don't think that saved him from anything."
She looked at the old man's bad hand, the way it lay limp in his lap, and the good hand that kept shaking the cup.
"The eastern door," she said. "Who's been oiling the lock?"
Bhola did not answer right away. He looked toward the back window, where the courtyard lay in grey light.
"I do," he said. "Every Sunday. He asked me to keep the hinge from crying when it's opened. And every Sunday I leave a bowl of rice on the step and a tin of mustard oil beside it. In the morning the rice is gone and the tin is empty and the bowl is clean. I never see anyone. I don't go in." He breathed out. "Eleven years, Ishu-didi. I have stopped asking myself what I'm feeding."
"Rats," Ishita said.
"Rats don't close the oil tin."
---
By one in the afternoon the parts lay on a bedsheet on the study floor in a line, the way her father would have laid them out, the way the manual's diagram had shown them. Side plate with its four screws. Hammer with its spring. Trigger and sear. Cylinder, its five green rims and one clean chamber. Grips, two cracked panels. The frame, its trigger guard intact.
She sat cross-legged in front of them and opened the manual.
It was an Indian Army handbook with the cover half torn away, the pages gone translucent at the folds. Someone had underlined in pencil. The underlines were in her father's hand. She had not noticed them last night.
She began to build.
---
CHAPTER 5. Business
They came at half past three, in a white Bolero with a dented bonnet and mud to the axle.
Ishita heard the engine on the laterite road, the grinding whine, then the gate, then two doors closing with the flat slam of cheap steel. She was in the study with the bolt drawn. The parts lay on the sheet in front of her, but the sheet had moved: the frame was seated in the grips now, and the side plate was back on with two screws, and the hammer lay inside the frame waiting for its spring.
She stopped and listened.
Three men. One short and broad, in a grey safari suit dark under the arms, sunglasses pushed up on his forehead, face like a closed fist. One lean and young, with a folder under one arm and a pen behind his ear. And a boy in a cricket cap pulled low, his belt cinched too tight over his jeans.
She could see them through the jalousie. She could hear them through the wall.
"Bhola-kaka," the broad man called. His voice was surprisingly gentle. "Still alive. Good."
Bhola's voice came back, thin. "Dulal-babu."
"Dulal is enough. Babu is what the bank calls me." A pause. "Is the girl here?"
Ishita put the screwdriver on the sheet, very quietly, and picked up the hammer.
---
She went out onto the veranda because it was better than being found in the study, and because she wanted to see his face.
Dulal Mondal stood at the bottom of the steps with his hands in his pockets, as though he had come to inspect a plot of land he already owned. Up close he was older than she had expected, fifty perhaps, with close-cut grey at the temples and a belly that sat comfortably under the safari shirt. He smiled at her as though she were a child who had passed a small test.
"You have his face," he said. "The same stubbornness in the mouth."
"What do you want?"
"Business." He took one hand out of his pocket and gestured at the thin young man, who opened the folder and took out a sheet of heavy paper. "Solvere Bank is financing the Kalitola link road. You may have read about it. The state wants the road. The bank wants the title clean before it releases the money. There's an auditor in Durgapur on Monday, very thorough, very dull. He will look at the chain of title for every plot the road touches. Kalitola is the last plot."
"That's nothing to do with me."
"It's everything to do with you." He nodded at the paper. "This is the loan sheet. The bank's own risk model approved our tranche in June. You see here, the reason code. Relationship Coverage: Tier A. Nobody at the bank can tell you what Tier A means. Nobody has asked for four years." He smiled. "The auditor will ask. And when he does, the only thing that can answer him is a stub of paper that says a Roy Chowdhury paid a Mondal for a ferry, and a dead man's name is written on it."
Ishita said nothing.
"Your grandfather bought Kalitola with a bullet," Dulal said. "We're selling the road with a signature. We're the same, Ishita. We know what a balance is. We know a ledger that doesn't close is a debt somebody has to pay."
"I don't have any receipt."
"No?" He looked past her at the house. "Then you won't mind if we look."
The thin clerk stepped forward, and Bhola moved to stand in front of him, and the boy in the cricket cap shoved the old man aside. Bhola went down on the top step with a grunt, his hand pressed to his ribs. Ishita took a step toward him, and Dulal's hand closed on her arm, not roughly, the grip of a man who knew how much force was needed.
"Don't," he said. "I'm not here to hurt the old man. I'm here to get the paper back. You understand the difference?"
She twisted free.
"Pratap, take the back," Dulal said. "The clerk takes the kitchen. I'll take the hall. Ishita, you'll stay here where I can see you. Or you'll sit in your father's study and bolt the door, which is what I think you're going to do, because you're clever and you've already decided what I'm going to say."
She had decided. She went back into the house and bolted the study door, and heard Dulal laugh softly on the other side of it, and then his boots going away down the hall.
CHAPTER 6. Stacking
The search lasted two hours. She listened to all of it.
Drawers opening and closing. The clerk's voice reading out inventory in a flat tone, the way a bank officer reads a list of collateral. The grain store door slamming against the wall. Pratap's boots on the courtyard stones, and then on the veranda, pacing. The clock in the hall ticking, which it was not supposed to do. Bhola's breath, wet and slow, coming from somewhere near the sideboard.
She worked.
The hammer went into the frame first, with the mainspring compressed behind it. That was the hardest part. The spring wanted to fly. She had to hold the hammer back with her thumb, feel for the seat in the frame, and ease the spring into its housing a coil at a time, like coaxing an animal out from under a bed. The first time it slipped and cracked against the steel and she froze, breath held, listening for boots. The boots kept pacing. She tried again. It seated.
The sear went in next. She had seen the diagram a dozen times. It sat behind the hammer, a small angled piece of steel no bigger than a grain of rice, and it caught the hammer's tooth to hold it back until the trigger released. She fitted it, felt it engage, drew the hammer back half an inch with the thumb, and felt the sear hold. Then she released the hammer slowly, and it stopped at the right point, and she let out a breath she had not known she was holding.
The trigger slid into its slot with a click so small she almost missed it.
She checked the action with the manual open beside her. Cock, hold, release. The hammer fell cleanly, a flat dry click, on an empty frame. Her father's underlines said stacking, and she felt the heavy resistance of the double action and understood what he meant. It was not a pull. It was a negotiation.
The side plate went on with the four screws. She turned each one with the screwdriver's taped handle, her wrist aching, the threads biting cleanly. She counted them. Four. She did not feel the old reflex. She felt only the screws going home.
The grips were harder. They were cracked along one edge and the fit was not perfect. She filed the cracked edge with the rough side of the tin lid, a small grinding sound she tried not to make, and set the left panel against the frame. It seated with a little solid click. The right panel followed. The wood was cool and smooth against her palm.
The cylinder crane was the last thing. The cylinder had to be pinned to the crane and the crane had to lock into the frame, and she did not yet have the pin.
She searched the toolkit. She searched the bedsheet. Then she remembered the screwdriver in her father's shaving kit, and the tin in the almirah, and found under the brush a small brass pin with a split head, the same length as the gap in the crane. It had been waiting in the tin the whole time, the way the important things in this house always waited.
She fitted the pin. The cylinder swung on the crane with a dry click. She closed the breech and it locked.
The gun was whole.
She sat on the bedsheet, her hands flat on her thighs, her breath coming hard, and listened to Pratap pacing on the veranda, and to Dulal's voice in the hall saying something about the sideboard, and she thought of nothing at all.
Then the study door shook in its frame.
"Ishita," Dulal said. He had come back. "Open the door, please. I'd like to finish this like adults."
CHAPTER 7. Live Round
She did not answer. She picked up the gun.
The cylinder was empty. She had known it would be. The five green-rimmed rounds from the first afternoon were in the toolkit's inner pocket, wrapped in a strip of cloth. She took one out. The rim was dull and cracked a little at the edge. She held it to the lamp and looked at the primer, and felt her stomach turn over.
She fitted it into the chamber. It seated with a small resistant click.
"Ishita," Dulal said, softer now. "The boy's on the sill. He's stupid, but he's strong, and he's been asking me for ten minutes if he can go through the window. I've told him no. I'm running out of reasons."
The window glass came in with a crash.
Pratap's arm came through the broken pane and his fingers closed on the wooden frame. His cricket cap was gone. His face came up into the gap, white under a dark streak of blood, and he saw her standing in the lamplight with the gun in both hands, the barrel level with his eyes.
"Didi," he said, and laughed, a short high breathless sound, and it was the laugh of a boy who did not believe what he was seeing.
She had worked out the trigger pull in the lamplight, the long heavy stacking that the manual had underlined in her father's hand. She had felt the sear engage, held the hammer back, felt the tension in the spring. She did not think about it. She let the trigger come back through its long resistance and the hammer fell.
The sound was not a crack. It was a flat enormous punch of air that hit her in the chest and the ears and the teeth, and the study filled with a white sting of smoke, and the frame jumped in her hands like something that had been waiting eleven years to be asked. Her wrist snapped back. A sheet of plaster came down from the ceiling somewhere above her and filled the room with grit.
When the smoke thinned she could see the window. Pratap was gone from the sill.
He was screaming out in the courtyard, high and broken. Dulal's voice cut through it, sharp and fast, a single name. Pratap. Then a second voice she didn't know, deeper, saying something hurried. Then the screaming stopped being words and became a sound that went on and on.
The door shook. Someone threw their weight against it. The brass bolt groaned.
Then it stopped.
She stood in the smoke with the gun hot against her palm. She did not know whether she had hit him in the body or the shoulder. She knew only that the round had gone out with a force that shook the window frame, and that the cylinder had turned one chamber in the crane, and that four green rounds remained beside one clean chamber, and that the old rims would not stay quiet for ever.
"Ishita," Dulal said through the wood. His voice had changed. It was no longer soft. "That was a live round."
She said nothing.
"That gun was never supposed to fire again. Your grandfather knew it. Your father knew it. I know it." A pause, then something almost like respect. "You've shot a boy in the shoulder who wasn't afraid of the thing. Let's see how you handle the rest of us."
She heard him walk away from the door. She heard him say, in the courtyard, quietly, take him to the car, he's alive, don't let him bleed on the seat. She heard the Bolero's engine start on the gravel, and Pratap's voice rising in pain as the door slammed, and then the engine's whine going down the laterite road toward the crossing, and the road going quiet.
She did not move for a long time.
---
CHAPTER 8. Keep Both
Bhola was on the hall floor when she came out. He had slid down the sideboard and sat with his back to the carved legs, under the grandfather's portrait, his good hand pressed to his ribs. His face was grey with sweat.
"They've gone," he said. "Dulal walked the boy to the gate himself. He's a strange man. He pays the doctor in Bolpur every month for the widows, did you know that? And he burned a child's school in Kalitola last year because the father wouldn't sell." He breathed through his teeth. "Both things are true. That's the Mondals."
She knelt beside him. "You need a doctor."
"I need a bed and a bottle of rum that I'm not allowed to have." He smiled with half his mouth. "Stop looking at me like I'm going to die on your carpet. Look at the portrait."
She turned. The backing was still open at the corner where she had left it.
She had not put it back. She had been too busy with the gun.
The counterfoil was still in her shirt. She took it out and unfolded it again on the tiles beneath the lamp. The brown ink. The shakier line in the grandfather's hand. The shot was true. The old man will not speak again. Below it, the pencil note in her father's hand: Mondal has the original. This is ours. Keep both.
"He kept the stub," Ishita said. "Your grandfather. He kept the copy that proved what he paid for and what it bought, so nobody could tell the story without him."
"Yes."
"And my father found it."
"Your father found everything." Bhola leaned his head back against the sideboard. "He found the gun when he was nine. He took it apart once. He told me he was terrified of the parts, so he put them in separate places and wrote them down, so he could always put the house back the way it was. I think he thought if he did it right, nobody would ever have to put it back together."
"But somebody did."
"You did," said Bhola. "And the house has been waiting for someone who would."
She sat back on her heels and looked at the old man for a long time.
CHAPTER 9. Balance Nil
Then she stood, slowly, her knees stiff, and went to the back of the house, and took the lamp from the study table. She carried it out across the courtyard in the failing light, past the pukur with its dark mirror and the first stars sitting in it, and stood before the eastern wing.
The padlock was still on the hasp. It was clean and shining. Beside the step, on a flat stone, sat a steel bowl. It was empty and polished. Beside it a tin of mustard oil, its lid carefully closed, a rag folded neatly beside it.
She noticed the slippers last. A pair of rubber chappals, cracked at the heel and worn thin at the toe, sitting on the threshold where the sill met the step, side by side, the way someone leaves shoes outside a door when they have come in from the rain and do not intend to go out again soon.
She knew the left heel, because she had seen it in the photograph her mother kept on the bedroom wall in Kolkata. Her father, on a beach she did not remember, one foot up on a rock, laughing at the camera, the cracked heel showing.
She crouched and touched the nearer slipper. It was cold, and damp, and when she pressed it, the rubber gave a little under her finger.
Above the wing, somewhere over a roof that did not exist, four steps. Unhurried. Careful.
A pause.
Four steps back.
She did not move. She did not count. She held the lamp steady against the dark and thought of her father standing at the pukur every evening, throwing stones into the water, never once in the same place, looking for the one that would sink without a sound.
The footsteps came again. Four. Pause. Four.
Ishita lifted the lamp higher, so that its light fell along the strip of darkness beneath the door, and she said, into the still air, in a voice that did not shake, "I know you're there, Baba."
The steps stopped.
For a long moment there was nothing at all. Then, from the other side of the wood, very soft, a single click: the sound of a hammer falling on an empty chamber.
And from the dark behind the door, the voice she had not heard in eleven years answered her, in the flat, careful tone of a man reading out a figure he had checked three times.
"Balance nil, Ishu."
